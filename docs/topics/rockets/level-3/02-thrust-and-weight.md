# Thrust and weight

*Rockets and landing · level 3, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

The Saturn V weighed about 29,000 kN on the launch pad, and its engines pushed with about 34,000 kN. How much push was
left over to lift it?

## The idea

Two forces act on a rocket at lift-off:

- its **weight**, pulling down;
- its **thrust**, pushing up.

Take one from the other to find the **net force**, the push that is left over:

$$\text{net force} = \text{thrust} - \text{weight}.$$

- If the net force is **positive** (thrust bigger), the rocket speeds up upwards.
- If it is **zero**, the rocket hovers, or keeps going at a steady speed.
- If it is **negative** (weight bigger), the rocket cannot lift off.

**The thrust-to-weight ratio** compares them: thrust divided by weight. A rocket needs more than 1 to lift off. Big
rockets launch at about 1.2 to 1.5: enough to rise, but not so much that they need huge engines. A rocket on Mars, where
the weight is only 38% as much, could lift off with much smaller engines.

## Worked examples

**1. Saturn V.** $34{,}000 - 29{,}000 = 5{,}000$ kN net, upwards. Thrust-to-weight ratio: $34{,}000 \div 29{,}000
\approx 1.2$.

**2. A model rocket.** Mass 0.1 kg, so weight about 1 N. Its motor gives 6 N. Net force $6 - 1 = 5$ N up, and the
thrust-to-weight ratio is 6: it leaps off the pad.

**3. The same rocket on Mars.** A rocket weighing 1,000 kN on Earth weighs only 380 kN on Mars. Engines of 500 kN would
not lift it on Earth, but on Mars the net force is $500 - 380 = 120$ kN up.

## Try it

1. A rocket weighs 500 kN and its engines push 600 kN. Find the net force. Will it lift off?
2. A rocket weighs 800 kN and its engines push 750 kN. What happens?
3. Find the thrust-to-weight ratio of a rocket with 1,200 kN of thrust and 1,000 kN of weight.
4. A lander hovers above Mars. Its weight there is 4,000 N. What is its thrust?

??? note "Answers"
    1. $600 - 500 = 100$ kN up. Yes.
    2. The weight is bigger, so it stays on the pad.
    3. $1{,}200 \div 1{,}000 = 1.2$.
    4. 4,000 N: to hover, thrust equals weight.

## Common mistake

**"The rocket lifts off as soon as the engines start."** The engines must first push harder than the whole weight.
Big rockets are often held down by clamps for a few seconds while the engines build up to full thrust.
