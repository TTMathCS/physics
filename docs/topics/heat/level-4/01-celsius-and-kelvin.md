# Celsius and kelvin

*Heat and matter · level 4, lesson 1 of 6*

## A question to start

NASA's fact sheet gives Mars's average temperature as 214 K. What is a K, and is 214 hot or cold?

## The idea

The **Celsius** scale is set by water: 0 °C where it freezes and 100 °C where it boils (at Earth's sea-level air
pressure).

But there is a coldest possible temperature, where particles have the least energy they can have. It is
**absolute zero**, −273.15 °C. Nothing can be colder.

The **kelvin** scale starts there. Its steps are the same size as Celsius degrees, so

$$T\ (\text{K}) = T\ (°\text{C}) + 273.15.$$

There is no degree sign: you write 214 K, "214 kelvin". In the United States, temperatures are often in **Fahrenheit**:
$T\ (°\text{F}) = 1.8\,T\ (°\text{C}) + 32$.

## Worked examples

**1. Mars to Celsius.** $214 - 273 = -59$ °C.

**2. Body heat to kelvin.** $37 + 273 = 310$ K.

**3. A change.** Viking 1's site warmed from 184 K to 242 K: a rise of 58 K, which is also a rise of 58 °C.

**4. To Fahrenheit.** 20 °C is $1.8 \times 20 + 32 = 68$ °F.

## Try it

1. Convert 0 °C to kelvin.
2. Convert 300 K to °C.
3. Can anything be at −300 °C? Why?
4. Convert −40 °C to °F.

??? note "Answers"
    1. 273 K.
    2. 27 °C.
    3. No. That is below absolute zero, −273.15 °C, the coldest possible.
    4. $1.8 \times (-40) + 32 = -40$ °F. At −40 the two scales agree.

## Common mistake

**"Twice the temperature in °C means twice as hot."** 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C. Only in kelvin do ratios make
sense: 293 K against 283 K, about 4% more.
