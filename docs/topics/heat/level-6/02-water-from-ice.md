# Water from ice

*Heat and matter · level 6, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

On Earth, water comes out of a tap. On Mars, a base will dig up ice at about −60 °C. How much energy does it take to
turn a kilogram of that ice into a glass of water at room temperature?

## The idea

Three steps, each with its own formula:

1. **Warm the ice** from −60 °C to 0 °C: $Q_1 = m\,c_\text{ice}\,\Delta T$.
2. **Melt it** at 0 °C: $Q_2 = mL_f$.
3. **Warm the water** from 0 °C to 20 °C: $Q_3 = m\,c_\text{water}\,\Delta T$.

| Getting 1 kg of water at 20 °C | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it starts | ice dug from the ground, about −60 °C | a tap |
| Heat needed, about | 544 kJ (0.15 kWh) | almost none |

That does not count the energy to dig the ice, carry it and clean the water.

## Worked examples

**1. One kilogram.** $Q_1 = 1 \times 2100 \times 60 = 126{,}000$ J. $Q_2 = 1 \times 334{,}000 = 334{,}000$ J.
$Q_3 = 1 \times 4180 \times 20 = 83{,}600$ J. Total 543,600 J, about 0.15 kWh.

**2. A day's top-up.** Most of a base's water would be cleaned and used again. Suppose it must still melt 1,000 kg of
new ice a sol: $1000 \times 0.15 = 150$ kWh, about 6 kW day and night.

**3. Using waste heat.** A reactor or power unit makes far more spare heat than this. Piping it to the ice-melter turns
waste into water.

## Try it

1. Which of the three steps takes the most energy?
2. Find the energy to turn 3 kg of ice at −20 °C into water at 0 °C.
3. How much energy turns 1 kg of ice at −60 °C into steam at 100 °C? (Use $L_v$ = 2,260 kJ/kg.)
4. Why would a Mars base clean and reuse its water rather than melt new ice?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Melting: 334 kJ of the 544 kJ.
    2. $3 \times 2100 \times 20 + 3 \times 334{,}000 = 126{,}000 + 1{,}002{,}000 = 1.13\times10^6$ J.
    3. $126 + 334 + 418 + 2260 = 3{,}138$ kJ.
    4. Melting, digging and cleaning take a lot of energy and work; cleaning used water is cheaper.

## Common mistake

**Missing a step.** Ice at −60 °C must first be warmed to 0 °C before it can melt. Leaving out a step, or using the
wrong $c$ (ice's is half of water's), gives the wrong total.
