# Deep water on Mars

*Air and fluids · level 5, lesson 3 of 6*

## A question to start

Mars once had lakes and flowing rivers, and future bases may hold big tanks of water. How deep would you have to dive in
a Mars tank to feel the same pressure as standing in Earth's air?

## The idea

The pressure under water is $p = \rho g h$. Water's density is the same on Mars. But $g$ is smaller: 3.71 N/kg against
9.81 N/kg. So each metre of water adds only about **38%** as much pressure on Mars.

| Under water | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gravity, $g$ | 3.71 N/kg | 9.81 N/kg |
| Extra pressure for each metre of water | 3,710 Pa | 9,810 Pa |
| Air pressure at the surface | 636 Pa | 101,400 Pa |
| Depth to reach a total of 101,400 Pa | about 27 m | at the surface |

## Worked examples

**1. 10 m down on Mars.** $1000 \times 3.71 \times 10 = 37{,}100$ Pa from the water.

**2. The same extra pressure.** On Earth, 10 m adds 98,100 Pa. On Mars that needs $98{,}100/(1000 \times 3.71) = 26.4$ m.

**3. Earth's air pressure, under Mars water.** Total $= 636 + 3710h = 101{,}400$, so $h = 100{,}764/3710 = 27.2$ m.

## Try it

1. Why does each metre of water add less pressure on Mars?
2. Find the pressure from 5 m of water on Mars.
3. A Mars base tank is 4 m deep. What is the total pressure at its bottom if the air inside the base is at 70,000 Pa?
4. A diver on Earth goes 30 m down. How deep would they go on Mars for the same pressure from the water alone?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Gravity is weaker, so the water above weighs less.
    2. $1000 \times 3.71 \times 5 = 18{,}550$ Pa.
    3. $70{,}000 + 1000 \times 3.71 \times 4 = 84{,}840$ Pa.
    4. $30 \times 9.81/3.71 \approx 79$ m.

## Common mistake

**"Water is lighter on Mars, so it is less dense."** Its mass per cubic metre, its density, is the same everywhere. Only
its weight changes.
