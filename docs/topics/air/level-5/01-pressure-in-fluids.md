# Pressure in fluids

*Air and fluids · level 5, lesson 1 of 6*

## A question to start

A mechanic lifts a whole car with one hand on a small hydraulic jack. How can a small push lift something so heavy?

## The idea

**Pressure** is force on each square metre, $p = F/A$, measured in pascals (Pa).

In a liquid or gas at rest, pressure acts **equally in every direction** at each point. And if you push on a liquid in a
closed system, the extra pressure spreads to **every part** of it. This is **Pascal's principle**.

A hydraulic jack uses it. A small piston pushes on oil; the pressure spreads to a big piston:

$$\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}.$$

The big piston has a bigger area, so the same pressure gives a bigger force.

## Worked examples

**1. The jack.** The small piston is 0.0005 m², the big one 0.05 m², 100 times bigger. A push of 150 N gives a pressure of
$150/0.0005 = 300{,}000$ Pa, and the big piston pushes with $300{,}000 \times 0.05 = 15{,}000$ N, enough to lift a car's
corner.

**2. The cost.** Energy is not free. To lift the big piston by 1 cm, the oil from the small piston must fill 100 times
the area by 1 cm, so the small piston must move 100 cm.

**3. Car brakes.** Your foot pushes a small piston; the pressure spreads through the brake fluid to bigger pistons at
each wheel that squeeze the brake pads.

## Try it

1. State Pascal's principle.
2. A jack's pistons are 0.001 m² and 0.04 m². What force on the small one lifts 8,000 N on the big one?
3. In question 2, how far must the small piston move to raise the load 2 cm?
4. Why must the oil in a jack have no air bubbles in it?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Extra pressure on a fluid at rest in a closed system spreads to every part of it.
    2. $8{,}000 \times 0.001/0.04 = 200$ N.
    3. 40 times as far: 80 cm.
    4. Air squashes easily, so the push would squash the bubbles instead of moving the big piston.

## Common mistake

**"A jack makes energy."** It gives a bigger force over a smaller distance. Force times distance, the work, is the same
on both sides (less a little lost to friction).
