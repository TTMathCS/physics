# Pressure and depth

*Air and fluids · level 5, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

A dam wall is much thicker at the bottom than at the top. Divers' ears hurt as they go deeper. Why does the pressure
grow with depth?

## The idea

Under a liquid, the pressure comes from the weight of the liquid above. Take a column of liquid of height $h$ over an area
$A$. Its volume is $Ah$, its mass $\rho A h$ (where $\rho$, "rho", is the density), and its weight $\rho A h g$. The
pressure is weight over area:

$$p = \rho g h.$$

This is the extra pressure from the liquid. The total pressure also includes the air pressing on the top:

$$p_\text{total} = p_\text{air} + \rho g h.$$

Pressure depends only on the **depth**, not the shape of the container. A narrow pipe of water 10 m tall pushes on its
base with the same pressure as a lake 10 m deep.

## Worked examples

**1. 10 m of water on Earth.** $p = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 10 = 98{,}100$ Pa, nearly as much again as the whole air
above.

**2. The total.** $101{,}400 + 98{,}100 = 199{,}500$ Pa, about twice sea-level pressure. Every 10 m down adds about one
more "air's worth".

**3. A water tank.** A tank 2 m deep: $1000 \times 9.81 \times 2 = 19{,}600$ Pa at the bottom from the water.

## Try it

1. Why does pressure grow with depth?
2. Find the water pressure (not counting the air) 25 m down in the sea. Take seawater as 1,025 kg/m³ and $g = 9.81$ N/kg.
3. Why is a dam thicker at the bottom?
4. A thin tube and a wide tank both hold water 3 m deep. Which has more pressure at the bottom?

??? note "Answers"
    1. There is more liquid above, pressing down.
    2. $1025 \times 9.81 \times 25 \approx 251{,}000$ Pa.
    3. The water pressure is greatest at the bottom.
    4. Neither: the pressure is the same, because the depth is the same.

## Common mistake

**"More water means more pressure."** It is the depth that counts, not the amount. A huge shallow lake pushes less on its
floor than a narrow deep well.
