# No air to breathe

*Air and fluids · level 4, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Climbers on Mount Everest often carry bottled oxygen, even though a fifth of the air there is still oxygen. If that is
too little, how much oxygen is there to breathe on Mars?

## The idea

What your lungs care about is how hard the **oxygen alone** pushes. Each gas in a mixture pushes with its share of the
total pressure:

$$\text{oxygen's push} = \text{oxygen's share} \times \text{total pressure}.$$

Scientists call this the **partial pressure** of oxygen.

| Oxygen's push | Mars | Earth, sea level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total pressure | 636 Pa | 101,400 Pa |
| Oxygen's share | 0.16% | 20.95% |
| Oxygen's push | about 1 Pa | about 21,200 Pa |

On the top of Everest, oxygen's push is about $0.21 \times 33{,}000 \approx 6{,}900$ Pa, a third of sea level. On
Mars it is about 1 Pa: about **21,000 times less** than at sea level.

## Worked examples

**1. Sea level.** $0.2095 \times 101{,}400 \approx 21{,}200$ Pa.

**2. Mars.** $0.0016 \times 636 \approx 1.0$ Pa.

**3. How many times less?** $21{,}200 \div 1.0 \approx 21{,}000$ times.

## Try it

1. What is the partial pressure of a gas?
2. Find oxygen's push in air at 50,000 Pa that is 21% oxygen.
3. In a Mars base, the air is at 70,000 Pa and 30% oxygen. Find oxygen's push. Is it more or less than at sea level?
4. Why could a person not breathe Mars air, even with a pump to squeeze it?

??? note "Answers"
    1. The push of that gas alone: its share times the total pressure.
    2. $0.21 \times 50{,}000 = 10{,}500$ Pa.
    3. $0.30 \times 70{,}000 = 21{,}000$ Pa, about the same as at sea level.
    4. Squeezed up to Earth pressure, it would still be 95% carbon dioxide and only 0.16% oxygen. The oxygen would first
       have to be made, by splitting the carbon dioxide.

## Common mistake

**"If the air is a fifth oxygen, there is plenty."** It depends on the total pressure too. On Everest the share is the
same as at sea level, but the push is a third.
