# The sol

*Space and time · level 4, lesson 1 of 6*

## A question to start

After Perseverance landed in 2021, about 350 people at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory worked on Mars time. Their
first shift started at 2 p.m. When did it start a week later?

## The idea

A Mars solar day, from noon to noon, is called a **sol**. On average it lasts **24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds**.

| Length of a day | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noon to noon, on average | 24 h 39 min 35 s | 24 h |

Since the Viking landers of 1976, mission clocks split the sol into **24 Mars hours**, each of 60 Mars minutes. So a
Mars hour is a little longer than an Earth hour: about **61.6 Earth minutes**.

The extra 39 and a half minutes adds up. If you follow Mars time, your day starts about 40 minutes later on the Earth
clock each day. After about **36 days** the drift has gone all the way round the clock, and you are back where you
started.

## Worked examples

**1. A Mars hour.** $24 \text{ h } 39.6 \text{ min} = 1{,}479.6$ minutes. $1{,}479.6 \div 24 \approx 61.6$ Earth minutes.

**2. A week of drift.** Each day the start time slips about 40 minutes. After 7 days: $7 \times 40 = 280$ minutes,
about 4 hours 40 minutes. A shift that started at 2 p.m. starts at about 6:40 p.m. a week later.

**3. Round the clock.** A full day is 1,440 minutes. $1{,}440 \div 39.6 \approx 36$ days.

## Try it

1. What is a sol?
2. How much longer is a sol than an Earth day?
3. Is a Mars hour longer or shorter than an Earth hour?
4. You follow Mars time for 3 days. About how much later does your day start on the Earth clock?

??? note "Answers"
    1. A solar day on Mars, noon to noon.
    2. About 39 and a half minutes.
    3. Longer: about 61.6 Earth minutes.
    4. $3 \times 40 = 120$ minutes, about 2 hours later.

## Common mistake

**"A sol is 24 hours 37 minutes."** That is how long Mars takes to turn once against the stars. A sol, noon to noon, is
a couple of minutes longer. The next lesson shows why.
