# Beta

*Radiation and the atom · level 5, lesson 3 of 6*

## A question to start

There are no electrons in a nucleus. Yet some nuclei shoot out fast electrons. Where do they come from?

## The idea

In **beta decay**, a neutron inside the nucleus turns into a proton, and a fast **electron** is shot out. That electron
is the **beta particle**.

- The nucleus gains a proton: the atomic number goes **up by 1**.
- It has one neutron fewer and one proton more, so the mass number **stays the same**.

$${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \;\to\; {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + \text{electron}$$

(A tiny, almost undetectable particle called an antineutrino comes out too.)

A beta particle is about 7,000 times lighter than an alpha particle. It travels further, up to a few metres in air, and
can get into the skin. A sheet of plastic or a few millimetres of aluminium stops it. Tritium, carbon-14 and
strontium-90 are beta sources.

## Worked examples

**1. Carbon-14.** $Z$: $6 + 1 = 7$, which is nitrogen. $A$ stays 14. So carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14.

**2. Tritium.** Hydrogen-3 has 1 proton and 2 neutrons. After beta decay it has 2 protons and 1 neutron: helium-3.

**3. Potassium-40.** Most potassium-40 nuclei decay by beta: $Z$ goes from 19 to 20, which is calcium. So it becomes
calcium-40.

## Try it

1. What is a beta particle?
2. What happens inside the nucleus in beta decay?
3. Strontium-90 has 38 protons. What does it become after beta decay?
4. What would stop beta particles?

??? note "Answers"
    1. A fast electron shot out of a nucleus.
    2. A neutron turns into a proton.
    3. $Z = 39$, yttrium, with $A = 90$: yttrium-90.
    4. A sheet of plastic or a few millimetres of aluminium.

## Common mistake

**"Beta decay takes an electron from around the atom."** The beta particle is made in the nucleus, when a neutron turns
into a proton. The electrons around the atom are not involved.
