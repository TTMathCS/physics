# The trip to Mars

*Radiation and the atom · level 6, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Curiosity's radiation detector measured the dose inside the spacecraft every day of its flight to Mars. How does a day
of that compare with a year at home?

## The idea

Inside its spacecraft, Curiosity's detector measured an average of **1.8 mSv a day** from galactic cosmic rays during
the cruise. That is about the same as a whole year of natural radiation in Canada, every single day.

| Dose | Per day | Per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural radiation in Canada | about 0.005 mSv | 1.8 mSv |
| On the International Space Station, average | about 0.4 mSv | 150 mSv |
| Between Earth and Mars | 1.8 mSv | about 660 mSv |

The SwRI team worked out that the two trips of a Mars mission, out and back, with today's rockets, would give about
**0.66 Sv**, or 660 mSv, before counting any time on Mars.

## Worked examples

**1. Curiosity's trip.** 253 days at 1.8 mSv a day: $253 \times 1.8 \approx 455$ mSv.

**2. Days to the worker limit.** $50 \div 1.8 \approx 28$ days. In under a month, a crew would get the dose a nuclear
worker on Earth may get in a whole year.

**3. A faster trip.** Suppose new rockets cut each trip to 120 days. $240 \times 1.8 = 432$ mSv for the two, instead of
about 660.

## Try it

1. Roughly how many years of Canadian natural radiation does one day in deep space equal?
2. Find the dose for a 200-day trip at 1.8 mSv a day.
3. How much of the dose on the cruise came from solar particles, according to the Curiosity team?
4. Why does a shorter trip help more than thicker walls?

??? note "Answers"
    1. About one year.
    2. $200 \times 1.8 = 360$ mSv.
    3. About 5%.
    4. Walls barely slow galactic cosmic rays, but the dose adds up day by day, so fewer days means less dose.

## Common mistake

**"The space station is in space, so it gets the same dose."** The station orbits inside Earth's magnetic field, and
Earth blocks about a third of its sky. Between the planets there is neither.
