# How shields work

*Radiation and the atom · level 7, lesson 1 of 6*

## A question to start

A sheet of paper stops alpha particles dead, yet a metre of concrete does not stop every gamma ray. Why do the two
behave so differently?

## The idea

A shield takes energy away from radiation. How it does this depends on the radiation.

- **Charged particles** (alpha, beta, slow protons) pull on electrons all along their path, knocking them out of atoms
  and losing a little energy each time. They slow steadily and stop at a fairly definite depth, their **range**.
- **Gamma rays and X-rays** have no charge. Each photon goes on untouched until, by chance, it hits something and is
  absorbed or knocked aside. So the beam fades by the same share in each layer, and there is no range.
- **Neutrons** have no charge either. They lose energy by bumping into nuclei. They lose the most when they hit
  something of their own mass, so hydrogen-rich water and plastic are the best neutron shields.
- **Very fast cosmic rays** can smash nuclei in the shield. The pieces fly on as **secondary radiation**, which can
  itself be harmful.

| Radiation | How it loses energy | Good shield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha | ionising, along a short track | paper, skin |
| Beta | ionising, along a longer track | plastic, a few mm of aluminium |
| Gamma | photon by photon, by chance | lead, thick concrete |
| Neutrons | bumping into nuclei | water, plastic |

## Worked examples

**1. Alpha in air.** An alpha particle travels only a few centimetres in air before it has lost all its energy.

**2. Gamma.** A beam that loses half in each 0.86 cm of lead keeps $\tfrac12$ after one layer, $\tfrac14$ after two,
$\tfrac18$ after three. It never quite reaches zero.

**3. Neutrons.** A neutron that hits a hydrogen nucleus head-on can hand over nearly all its energy at once, like one
snooker ball striking another of the same mass. Hitting a heavy lead nucleus, it just bounces off, keeping almost all
its energy.

## Try it

1. Why do charged particles have a range, but gamma rays do not?
2. Why are water and plastic good neutron shields?
3. What is secondary radiation?
4. Which shield from the table would you choose for a beta source?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Charged particles lose energy steadily all along the path. Gamma photons are removed one at a time, by chance.
    2. They are rich in hydrogen, whose nucleus has about the same mass as a neutron.
    3. Particles knocked out when fast radiation smashes nuclei in the shield.
    4. Plastic, or a few millimetres of aluminium.

## Common mistake

**"The densest shield is always best."** Dense lead is good for gamma rays. Against neutrons and cosmic rays, light,
hydrogen-rich materials do better, as lesson 4 shows.
