# Starting and stopping

*Forces and motion · level 1, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

A toy car sits on the floor. Will it start rolling on its own? And once it is rolling, why does it stop?

## The idea

Things do not start moving on their own. A ball on the grass stays there until somebody kicks it. To **start**
something moving, you need a push or a pull.

Stopping needs a force too. When you catch a ball, your hands push against it to stop it. When a bike stops, the
brakes rub on the wheels. A toy car rolling on the carpet stops because the carpet rubs on its wheels. That rubbing
is a force too, and it is called **friction**.

If there were nothing to rub on and no air in the way, a moving thing would keep going for ever. Far out in space,
that is just what happens: a spacecraft that is moving keeps moving, without its engines on, for years.

## Worked examples

**1. A ball on ice.** A puck slides a long way on ice, because ice is slippery and rubs on it only a little. On grass,
a ball stops sooner: grass rubs a lot.

**2. Catching.** To stop a ball, you push back on it with your hands. A hard throw needs a bigger push to stop.

**3. A Mars rover.** A rover on Mars stops when its motors stop turning its wheels and hold them still. It does not
roll on by itself on flat ground.

## Try it

1. What do you need to start a ball rolling?
2. Why does a rolling toy car stop on a carpet?
3. Does a moving spacecraft far out in space need its engines to keep going?
4. What do your hands do to stop a ball you catch?

??? note "Answers"
    1. A push (or a pull).
    2. The carpet rubs on the wheels. The rubbing, called friction, is a force that slows it.
    3. No. With nothing to rub on, it keeps going.
    4. They push against it, the opposite way to how it is moving.

## Common mistake

**"Things stop because they run out of push."** A moving thing does not carry a push inside it that gets used up. It
stops because something else, like rubbing or a hand, pushes against it.
