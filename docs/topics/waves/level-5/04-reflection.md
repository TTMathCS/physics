# Reflection

*Waves, sound and light · level 5, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Ships measure the depth of the sea by sending a pulse of sound down and timing its echo. How does that work, and what
law does a reflected wave follow?

## The idea

When a wave hits a surface, it can **reflect**. Draw a line at right angles to the surface where the wave hits: the
**normal**. Then the **law of reflection** is:

$$\text{angle of incidence} = \text{angle of reflection},$$

both measured from the normal.

Timing an echo gives a distance. The wave goes there **and back**, so

$$d = \frac{v t}{2}.$$

This is how **sonar** (sound in water) and **radar** (radio waves) measure distance.

## Worked examples

**1. The angle.** A ray hits a mirror at 30° to the normal. It reflects at 30° to the normal, on the other side.

**2. Sonar.** Sound travels at 1,500 m/s in seawater. The echo returns after 0.4 s: $d = 1500 \times 0.4/2 = 300$ m.

**3. Radar to the Moon.** A radar pulse returns from the Moon after 2.56 s: $d = 3.00\times10^8 \times 2.56/2 =
3.84\times10^8$ m, about 384,000 km.

## Try it

1. What is the normal?
2. A light ray hits a mirror at 55° to the normal. At what angle does it reflect?
3. A bat hears the echo of its squeak 0.02 s later, in air at 340 m/s. How far away is the moth?
4. Why do you divide by 2 when you work out a distance from an echo?

??? note "Answers"
    1. A line at right angles to the surface where the wave hits.
    2. 55° to the normal.
    3. $340 \times 0.02/2 = 3.4$ m.
    4. The wave travels there and back, twice the distance.

## Common mistake

**Measuring angles from the surface.** Angles of incidence and reflection are measured from the **normal**, not from the
mirror.
