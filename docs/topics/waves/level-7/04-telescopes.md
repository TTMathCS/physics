# Telescopes

*Waves, sound and light · level 7, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Galileo's first telescopes, around 1610, were made of two small lenses in a tube. How do two lenses make faraway
things look closer?

## The idea

A simple **refracting telescope** has two converging lenses:

1. The **objective**, with a long focal length $f_o$, makes a real image of the distant object at its focal point.
2. The **eyepiece**, with a short focal length $f_e$, acts as a magnifying glass on that image.

With the two focal points together, the lenses are $f_o + f_e$ apart, and the **angular magnification** is

$$M = \frac{f_o}{f_e}.$$

The telescope makes things look $M$ times bigger in angle.

The size of the objective, the **aperture**, matters too. A wider lens or mirror catches more light, so faint things
show up, and it can separate finer detail. Big modern telescopes use curved **mirrors** instead of lenses, because large
mirrors are easier to make and support.

## Worked examples

**1. Magnification.** $f_o = 1.0$ m, $f_e = 0.025$ m: $M = 1.0/0.025 = 40$.

**2. Length.** The tube is about $1.0 + 0.025 = 1.025$ m long.

**3. Changing eyepieces.** Swap to $f_e = 0.010$ m: $M = 100$. But the image is dimmer and blurrier, because the same
light is spread over a bigger view.

## Try it

1. Which lens of a telescope has the longer focal length?
2. Find $M$ for $f_o = 0.90$ m and $f_e = 0.018$ m.
3. Why does a telescope with a bigger aperture show fainter stars?
4. Why do the largest telescopes use mirrors rather than lenses?

??? note "Answers"
    1. The objective.
    2. $0.90/0.018 = 50$.
    3. It collects more light.
    4. Large mirrors are easier to make and to support than large lenses.

## Common mistake

**"More magnification always shows more."** Beyond a point, extra magnification only makes a blurry image bigger. The
aperture limits how much detail there is to see.
